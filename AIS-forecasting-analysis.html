The task is to predict demand for the coming months, per branch and per SKU. This document sets out the method we will use and why, the platform it runs on, and what it costs. The approach is settled rather than speculative: we have already built and validated it against your own history. What remains is to run it in your tenancy, on a schedule, writing output your planners can use.

The four facts that shaped the design

1. Demand is a power law — 61 of 2,260 SKUs make half the volume, so the model only has to be excellent on a small core and merely sensible on the tail. 2. Aggregate demand is stable and mildly seasonal, but individual series are 62% intermittent, so accuracy is quoted per segment and never as one headline number. 3. 43% of stock value sits where there has been no demand for six months, while 20,945 combinations with live demand hold zero stock — a placement problem a forecast fixes. 4. One training run holds its accuracy across six months, which is what makes a small, cheap, quarterly-cadence platform the right answer rather than a compromise.

02

Demand structure

The parameters that decide what kind of model is appropriate. Several of these span orders of magnitude, so they are drawn on logarithmic axes — on a linear axis the tail that carries most of the SKUs would be an invisible smear against the baseline.

Demand is a power law

Ranking all 2,260 SKUs by volume produces an almost perfectly straight line on log-log axes, the signature of a power-law distribution. 61 SKUs — 2.7% of the range — make half of all units. 297 make 80%. The bottom 1,049 SKUs together account for 1% of volume. The practical consequence is that a model only has to be genuinely good on a few hundred series; the tail should be managed by policy, not prediction.

Two populations

On the Syntetos–Boylan scheme (§6), 14.9% of series are smooth and carry 80% of value; 62.4% are intermittent and carry 14.5%. Median average demand interval is 4.0 months and median CV² of non-zero sizes is 0.13. Only 31.7% of series have twelve or more months of non-zero history — but those carry 92.0% of value.

Growth is steady, seasonality is shallow but repeatable

FY 25-26 grew 7.9% in volume and 9.4% in value on FY 24-25. The seasonal index runs 86 (April) to 106 (June–July), and the two years' profiles correlate at r = 0.886. Because the swings are small relative to the level, the chart below omits the empty space beneath the data and adds a year-on-year panel — on a zero-based axis these movements would be invisible.

Useful covariates already present in the data

The substitution master carries attributes that map onto 99.8% of demand and are strong grouping variables for a global model — particularly vehicle age category, which is the closest available proxy for the size of the vehicle parc in each replacement cohort.

03

Service and stock: what the forecast is for

Two measurements that establish why a forecast is worth building here, and both come from comparing files rather than reading any one of them.

One in five ordered units is never shipped

Across sixteen months branches ordered 2,602,392 units and received 2,133,961. The gap — 504,052 units, 19.4% of demand, roughly ₹166 crore at MRP — is visible only because the order file records both numbers. Monthly shortfall sat between 10% and 17% through FY 2025-26, then stepped up sharply from March 2026 and reached 37.1% in June 2026.

This makes invoiced sales a censored signal

Sales record what was available to sell, not what was wanted. A model trained on sales learns the supply constraint and forecasts the stockout forward. Use the order book as the target where it exists (Apr 2025 onward) and treat the FY 2024-25 sales period as censored observations. This single choice matters more than the algorithm.

The stock is not short — it is in the wrong places

This is the finding that makes the forecast actionable. Branches hold a median 75 days of cover, and 32 of 53 hold more than 60 days. Yet 19.4% of orders go unfilled. Splitting the ₹20.91 crore of closing stock against the last six months of demand at the same branch×SKU explains why:

How much of the shortfall is placement, and how much is genuine shortage

Of 1,892 SKUs short somewhere in the network, the same SKUs held 94,522 units in stock elsewhere against 499,357 units short. So about 19% of the shortfall could be met by moving existing stock — that part is a forecasting and allocation problem, and it is worth roughly ₹31 crore of MRP a year. The remaining 81% does not exist anywhere in the network and needs upstream supply. Both conclusions matter: the forecast is worth building, and it is not sufficient on its own.

Lead times are short and stable

Order-to-despatch runs a median of 3 days, 95th percentile 6, maximum 27. Hub means sit in a narrow band: Kolkata 2.6 days, Pune 3.0, Dharuhera 3.2, Kadi 3.4, Chennai 3.5, Roorkee 3.5, with standard deviations of 1.3–1.8 days. Replenishment risk is therefore dominated by the review period, not transit — which is good news for a monthly forecast, and means moving to weekly review would cut safety stock further.

04

How the forecast works

Nine decisions define the method. Each one is settled, and each is here with the reason it was chosen — the supporting literature is in §6.

1. We forecast what was ordered, not what was shipped

The target variable is ordered quantity. This matters more here than in most engagements: 19.4% of ordered units were never despatched (§3), so a model trained on despatches learns to reproduce the stockouts and quietly recommends the stock levels that caused them. Cells where an order went short are flagged as censored, because the true demand there is at least what was ordered and possibly more.

2. One global model across all 63,210 series, always

A single model is trained on every series at once rather than one model per branch×SKU. With 24 monthly observations per series — 62% of them zero — there is not enough local history to fit anything per-series; pooled, there are 948,150 training rows. Sparse series borrow strength from similar ones through the shared branch, region, hub, glass-type and vehicle-class features.

This holds for pilots too

If the first phase is scoped to a few branches, scope the reporting, not the training. Train on all 63,210 series and publish a scorecard for the pilot branches only. We tested the alternative on your data: training on one branch alone cost 2.6 points of accuracy overall and 8.9 points on its slow-moving lines, and saved no meaningful time — the full training run is under ten minutes either way.

The same property means a new branch or a newly listed SKU is forecastable immediately, from its attributes, with no waiting period to accumulate history. Given churn across 2,260 SKU codes, this is a recurring practical benefit rather than an edge case.

3. Horizon is a feature, so one model covers all six months

Rather than chaining a one-month model into itself six times — which compounds its own errors — the number of months ahead is passed to the model as an input. One fitted model answers “what will this branch need in four months?” directly. This is the direct multi-horizon strategy, and it is the single design choice that makes one training run per quarter sufficient.

4. Twenty-nine features, all computable at the moment of forecast

History
  • Lags at 1–6, 9 and 12 months
  • Rolling mean and standard deviation over 3, 6, 12 months
  • Non-zero counts over 6 and 12 months
  • Length of the current run of zero months
  • Trend as the 3-month mean minus the 6-month mean
  • The same calendar month one year earlier
Attributes
  • Branch, region, zone, supply hub, branch tier
  • Product group and sub-group, OEM status
  • Vehicle class, glass type, vehicle age band
  • MRP as a continuous feature
  • Months ahead, and calendar month of the target

Nothing in the list uses information unavailable at the time the forecast is made. This is enforced in code by building features only from months strictly before the cut.

5. The method is routed by value class, not chosen once

No single method wins everywhere, and pretending otherwise costs accuracy. A-class series are smooth and high-volume, where classical exponential smoothing is genuinely hard to beat; B and C are sparse, where the pooled model's ability to borrow across series pays off.

6. The output is a set of stocking levels, not a single number

A point forecast cannot answer the question replenishment actually asks, which is “how much do I hold to cover this line 90% of the time?” So four models are fitted — the central estimate plus the 80th, 90th and 95th percentiles — using a quantile loss. The planner consumes a service level; the quantile is the number that delivers it.

7. Forecasts are reconciled up the hierarchy

Individually accurate series forecasts do not add up to an accurate branch total, and branch totals do not add to a good national number. Because branch and national plans are what procurement actually uses, the forecasts are made coherent across the hierarchy using MinT reconciliation with a shrinkage covariance estimate and a non-negativity constraint. Without this step the three levels of the business disagree with each other by construction.

8. Quantiles become order-up-to levels using each branch's own lead time

The final conversion multiplies the chosen quantile by the branch's replenishment lead time from Location Master — median 3 days, 95th percentile 6, but varying by hub — and subtracts stock on hand and on order. The deliverable a planner opens is a quantity to order, not a statistic.

9. Retraining is quarterly, and gated

The model is refitted once a quarter and scored against the frozen benchmarks before it is allowed to replace the incumbent. If a new model does not beat the standing one on the holdout, it is not registered. Monthly scoring continues against whichever model currently holds the registry tag.

Accuracy to plan for

These are measured figures from the validation run on your own history — trained through September 2025, scored against October 2025 to March 2026 with no retraining in between. Plan against them.

Quote accuracy at the level the decision is made

A single accuracy number for a network like this is meaningless, because error falls by a factor of twenty between an individual line and the national plan. Planners do not order month by month against a single-month forecast; they place a six-month plan, and month-to-month errors partly cancel within it.

The stocking levels stay calibrated

The most important property is not the point accuracy but whether a “90% service level” actually delivers 90% service six months after the model was fitted. It does.

How this must be reported

Per segment, always. A headline “the model is 62% accurate” is both true and useless: it averages an A-class line that can be ordered against with a C-class line that cannot. The scorecard is WMAPE by value class and by aggregation level, pinball loss for the quantiles, and achieved coverage against target — reported every month against the frozen baselines, so drift is visible before it becomes a stockout.

06

Why these choices

The reasoning and the published evidence behind each decision in §4, with sources. Where our own measurement on AIS data differs from the literature, that is said plainly.

Why demand is classified the way it is

The smooth / intermittent / erratic / lumpy split used throughout is the Syntetos–Boylan classification, which splits on average demand interval (ADI) and the squared coefficient of variation of non-zero demand sizes (CV²), at thresholds of ADI = 1.32 and CV² = 0.49 [1]. Kostenko and Hyndman later showed the optimal boundary between methods is non-linear rather than a rectangular cut, though the original scheme remains the practical standard [2]. The AIS panel sits at median ADI 4.0 and median CV² 0.13 — deeply intermittent, with low size variability.

Why not Croston, the classic intermittent-demand method

Croston's method (1972) separates a series into demand sizes and inter-arrival intervals, smooths each independently and forecasts their ratio [3]. Syntetos and Boylan showed this is positively biased and proposed the SBA correction, multiplying by (1 − α/2), which became the standard benchmark [4]. TSB updates demand probability in every period rather than interval, which handles obsolescence far better [5].

We tested this family and it was the weakest of the nine methods tried. That is not a contradiction of the literature but a mismatch of purpose: Croston estimates a demand rate per period, which is the right object for a continuous-review reorder point and the wrong one for the discrete monthly bucket a planner orders in. We therefore use TSB for obsolescence detection — flagging the 22,643 dead branch×SKU rows found in §3 — and not SBA for quantity forecasting.

Why one global model rather than one model per series

The M5 competition established the modern default for large retail panels: a single global gradient-boosted model trained across all series, sharing parameters. LightGBM featured in most top solutions in both tracks, and the best machine-learning method was 22.4% more accurate than the best statistical benchmark [6].

The deeper result is Montero-Manso and Hyndman, Principles and Algorithms for Forecasting Groups of Time Series: Locality and Globality, which proves global models are not restricted to related or homogeneous series [18]. A global model can always fit at least as well as a collection of local ones given enough capacity, and generalises better because it has far more data per parameter. Relatedness is not the mechanism; sample size is. With 24 observations per series, 62% of them zero, that argument is decisive here — and it is why §4.2 trains globally even for a scoped pilot.

The caveat that shapes §4.7

A robustness check on M5 found that global models “performed very well for the bottom levels, but lacked robustness when the bottom forecasts were added up to aggregate level” [7]. We reproduced this exactly: the pooled model wins on B- and C-class series but loses to plain exponential smoothing at branch level (10.9% vs 13.9%) and nationally (1.8% vs 7.5%). It is a known property of the method, not a defect — and it is why reconciliation is mandatory rather than a refinement.

One further caution on transferring M5 results: it ran on daily data with 1,941 observations per series. We have 24 monthly ones. The advantage of machine learning over statistical methods shrinks as history shortens, which is why our measured gap is modest and, on A-class, negative. Expecting an M5-scale improvement here would be a mistake.

Why forecasts are reconciled

Given that error falls from 38% to 1.8% between the base and national levels, forecasts must be made coherent across the hierarchy rather than simply summed. The standard is MinT (minimum trace) reconciliation, from Wickramasuriya, Athanasopoulos and Hyndman, JASA 114(526), 2019, which uses the covariance matrix of base forecast errors to produce coherent forecasts minimising total mean squared error [8]. A shrinkage estimator of that covariance is required in practice, because the full matrix is not estimable from 24 observations [9]. The non-negative variant matters here specifically, since reconciliation can otherwise produce negative demand [10].

Why the output is quantiles

Replenishment consumes a service level, not a point estimate. Gradient boosting with a pinball (quantile) loss produces these directly and scales to panels of this size [11]. To make coverage guarantees rigorous rather than empirical, Conformalized Quantile Regression (Romano, Patterson and Candès, NeurIPS 2019) wraps any quantile regressor and attains valid finite-sample coverage without distributional assumptions [12], with extensions to the time-series setting where independence fails [13]. Our raw quantiles were already calibrated to within four points at six months, so conformal wrapping is a phase-two refinement rather than a necessity.

Why quarterly retraining is enough

This single decision determines the cost structure in §8. The question is studied directly in Zanotti, On the retraining frequency of global models in retail demand forecasting (2025), which finds clear diminishing returns to frequent updates [14]. Our own measurement is the stronger evidence: 12% error growth over six months with no retraining at all. Quarterly is comfortably sufficient, and the monthly-retrain MLOps machinery that dominates most cost estimates is not needed.

What we assessed and rejected for phase one

Deep learning. DeepAR, N-BEATS, N-HiTS, TFT, PatchTST and TiDE all require long histories to justify their parameter counts [15]. With 24 monthly points there is very little temporal structure for an attention mechanism to exploit, while training and serving cost rise by one to two orders of magnitude.

Time-series foundation models are the more interesting option, because they need no training run at all. Chronos-Bolt is roughly 5% more accurate than the original Chronos, up to 250× faster and 20× more memory-efficient [16], and Chronos-2 adds covariate-informed zero-shot forecasting. These are worth a zero-shot evaluation in phase two — specifically for the 10,200 single-month series where no time-series model can be fitted at all. But published evidence on intermittent monthly data is thin and benchmarking practice is still contested [17]. An experiment, not a plan.

The data is small, so a database is the right home for it

The five source files total 150 MB and the modelling panel is 32 MB. This is not big data. The usual justifications for a data lake — volume, schema-on-read, unstructured content — apply to none of it, and a lake would impose file-handling work on a team that already knows SQL. So the design uses a relational database for the working data, with blob storage kept for one specific job.

Why a database, for silver and gold
  • Point lookups. “What is the forecast for this branch and this SKU?” is an indexed key lookup — trivial in SQL, slow against Parquet files.
  • The masters are relational. Location Master is 57 rows; Substitution Mapping is 2,417. These are dimension tables with foreign keys.
  • Constraints catch defects on load. A format check on HSN Code would have flagged the transposed column in the FY 25-26 sheet automatically, rather than it being found by hand.
  • Power BI, Excel and any future application connect natively, with no intermediate export step.
Why blob storage stays, for one job only
  • The original workbooks, byte for byte. This is what let us prove the column transposition was in the source rather than in our parsing. It is the audit trail and it must never be edited.
  • Costs about two cents a month and needs no server that has to be running.
  • Forecast output is also written as a dated Parquet file alongside the database table, so every month's run is independently reproducible.

A data lake and its medallion layering — the layering is a naming convention for transformation stages, not a technology; at 32 MB it is three schemas in the database. Azure Data Factory — Azure ML pipelines have their own schedules, so a separate orchestrator adds cost without adding capability at this cadence. Databricks — a 32 MB panel does not need a distributed engine. Microsoft Fabric — an F-capacity bills continuously whether or not you use it. GPU compute — nothing in §4 requires one. Managed online endpoint — an always-on endpoint costs more per month than the entire rest of this design; a monthly batch job needs no real-time serving.

The three flows

Flow 1 — Load
  1. Upload the source workbooks to blob — unchanged, immutable
  2. A pipeline job parses them, normalises keys, fixes the transposed columns and converts Excel serials, writing to staging
  3. Aggregate to the branch×SKU×month panel and join covariates → mart.panel, 1.5 M rows
  4. Constraint checks and a reconciliation against the source totals must both pass

Runs whenever a new extract arrives — monthly, in minutes.

Flow 2 — Train, quarterly
  1. Cluster scales 0 → 1 node on job submission
  2. Reads mart.panel, builds the direct multi-horizon training set (~950k rows)
  3. Fits the point model plus q80, q90 and q95
  4. Runs the rolling backtest and logs WMAPE by segment to MLflow
  5. Registers the model only if it beats the frozen baseline
  6. Cluster scales back to 0

Four times a year, about ten minutes each.

Flow 3 — Serve, monthly
  1. Schedule triggers the batch endpoint
  2. Scores all 63,210 series × 6 horizons × 4 quantiles
  3. Reconciles to branch and national totals
  4. Converts quantiles to order-up-to levels using Location Master lead times
  5. Upserts to mart.forecast and writes a dated Parquet copy to blob
  6. Power BI refreshes against the database

Once a month, about fifteen minutes.

Security and operations

Managed identity throughout — no connection strings in code. The data carries GSTIN, PAN and customer contact details: restrict the raw container and the raw schema, and mask these columns in anything shared with planners or embedded in a dashboard. Private endpoints if policy requires, adding about $37/month for five. Alerts on job failure and on input drift. Infrastructure as code from day one — the whole estate is a short Bicep file.

The two ways this budget gets blown

A compute cluster with min_nodes above zero costs $160/month doing nothing — ten times the entire base design. An always-on managed online endpoint costs $73–$300/month to answer a question that is asked once a month. Both are default-looking settings, and both are the most common Azure ML billing mistakes. Set a budget alert at $50 and either will surface immediately.

Open decisions

The modelling question is answered. What is still open is not engineering — it is a small number of decisions only AIS can make, each of which changes the design.

Questions for the business

  • Is the 19.4% shortfall an allocation problem or a supply shortage? Our analysis says about 19% of it could be covered by moving existing stock and 81% could not. Confirm this with the supply team, because it sets the realistic ceiling on the benefit.
  • Can we get a monthly stock history rather than a single snapshot? This is the highest-value additional data, and it is almost certainly already being produced.
  • Is vehicle parc data available by geography? Demand peaks in the 9–15 year cohort, so a count of registered vehicles by model and age per catchment is the strongest covariate not currently in the data.
  • Is the substitution map complete? Only 132 of 2,417 SKUs have a substitute. Either the map is unfinished or substitution genuinely does not happen — these imply very different inventory policies.
  • What accuracy target will this be judged against? Agree the segment-level and quantile metrics in §5 now. A target of "95% SKU-level accuracy" cannot be met by any method on this data and would make an otherwise successful project look like a failure.
